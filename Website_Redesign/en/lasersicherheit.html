Laser safety

Laser classes explained

Laser radiation is concentrated, high-energy light. How hazardous a laser can be to the eyes and skin is described by its laser class – from Class 1 to Class 4.

Basics

Why laser classes exist

Unlike a lamp, a laser emits its light in a tightly concentrated beam. If it enters the eye, it can be focused onto a very small spot on the retina. Laser products are therefore divided into classes according to their hazard potential. The class determines which protective measures are required.

The classification is based on the standard IEC/EN 60825-1. For occupational safety in Germany, the Ordinance on Artificial Optical Radiation (OStrV) and the Technical Rules for Laser Radiation (TROS Laser Radiation) also apply.

The laser classes

From safe to very hazardous

Class 1

Safe

The accessible laser radiation is safe under reasonably foreseeable conditions of use – even when looking into the beam for longer. This also applies to devices in which a more powerful laser is fully enclosed.

Class 1M

Safe – without optical instruments

Like Class 1, as long as the beam is not viewed with optical instruments such as magnifiers, binoculars or telescopes. These can concentrate the radiation and make it hazardous.

Class 2

Visible and safe for short exposure

Visible radiation only (400–700 nm). A brief, accidental look into the beam (up to approx. 0.25 s) is considered safe for the eye. Deliberately staring into the beam must be avoided.

At BLAU: Thanks to pulsed operation, the MVpulse achieves up to 100 mW output power at laser Class 2.

Class 2M

Like Class 2 – without optical instruments

Visible radiation, safe for the naked eye during short exposure. With optical instruments such as magnifiers or binoculars, however, the radiation can become hazardous.

Class 3R

Hazardous to the eye

The accessible radiation can damage the eye; the risk is lower than with Class 3B. Looking directly into the beam must be avoided. Additional protective measures are required for operation.

Class 3B

Hazardous to the eye and – in some cases – the skin

The direct beam and specular reflections are hazardous to the eye. Depending on the power, the skin can also be affected. Laser safety goggles and controlled laser areas are required.

Class 4

Very hazardous to eye and skin

Very hazardous to the eye and hazardous to the skin. Diffusely scattered radiation can also be hazardous. The radiation can cause fires or explosions. The most extensive protective measures apply.

Important for Class 2 and 2M

Do not rely on the blink reflex

Classes 2 and 2M are based on the assumption that the eye closes or turns away from bright light within about 0.25 seconds. Studies show, however, that this reflex is not reliably triggered in everyone.

  • If a laser beam hits your eye: close your eyes deliberately and turn your head away immediately.
  • Infrared lasers (e.g. 785, 808, 850 nm) are invisible – there is no blink reflex. Particular caution is required.
  • Do not deliberately look into the beam or into specular reflections.

Protective measures

Working safely with lasers

The operator of the laser equipment is responsible for safe operation. The most important rules at a glance:

01

Do not look into the beam

Neither directly nor into specular reflections – and never with optical instruments such as magnifiers or binoculars.

02

Keep the beam away from eye level

Guide the laser beam clearly above or below eye level wherever possible and avoid reflections.

03

Label

Laser products must be labelled according to their class; laser areas are marked with warning signs.

04

Instruct

Employees working with lasers are instructed about the hazards of laser radiation and the protective measures.

05

From Class 3R: more protection

For Classes 3R, 3B and 4, a qualified laser safety officer and controlled, marked laser areas are required, among other things.

06

Protective equipment

Where required, the operator provides suitable laser safety goggles and further protective equipment.
